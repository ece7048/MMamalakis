                       Explainable Multiscale AI for Brain Cancer
                         From imaging phenotypes to treatment-resistant cellular states

My programme will address one biologically anchored question: can shared representations across MRI,
tissue architecture and gene regulation identify and mechanistically explain cellular states associated with
progression, relapse and treatment resistance in paediatric brain tumours? The initial disease focus will be
paedriatic ependymoma, Choroid Plexus Carcinoma (CPC), and medulloblastoma and adult glioma will
provide a later test of generalisability. This focus builds on nine years of research in medical imaging,
explainable AI and computational biology, and on my current work in paediatric brain cancer, foundation
models and cross-scale representation learning [1–6].
Central hypothesis. A cross-scale model that links imaging, histology and cellular programmes will predict
clinically or biologically defined tumour states more accurately and recover more stable, biologically
coherent features than matched unimodal models. The hypothesis will not be supported if the integrated
model fails to improve prespecified held-out performance and calibration, recover established tumour
signatures or known therapeutic targets, or produce explanations that remain stable across sites and
batches. However, the existing literature and evidence from multimodal AI modelling strongly support this
hypothesis.
Aim 1 — Explainable MRI foundation model for paediatric and adult brain tumours
Biological question: Which MRI-derived phenotypes reproducibly mark tumour state, progression and
treatment-related outcomes across paediatric and adult brain tumours?
Starting data: An existing 3D MRI foundation model trained on more than 425,000 structural and diffusion
scans; ABCD and UK Biobank pretraining cohorts; brain-tumour cohorts used in my Cambridge work;
Professor Stephen Price’s collected imaging dataset; and public Cancer Imaging Archive cohorts for
independent benchmarking. Only datasets with confirmed permissions and sufficiently defined labels will
enter each analysis.
Methodological advance: I will adapt the developed continual-learning and latent-diffusion framework to
neuro-oncology and pair it with explainability-first evaluation. Unlike a generic imaging encoder, the model
will be required to retain prior knowledge from large pretraining cohorts while adapting to paediatric and
adult brain tumours. It will produce sparse, stable and faithful explanations that identify anatomically
relevant MRI regions for subsequent investigation using histology and molecular data in Aims 2 and 3.
Benchmark and validation: Comparators will include supervised 3D CNN/transformer models, self-
supervised imaging encoders and single-site fine-tuning. Evaluation will use patient-level, site-held-out and
temporal splits where available, with calibration, bootstrapped confidence intervals and subgroup analysis.
Candidate MRI regions will be compared with known anatomy and available pathology, treatment and
outcome labels; claims beyond association will not be made.
Delivery, people and decision point: Postdoctoral scientist 1 and PhD student 1 will lead model adaptation
and interpretability. By year 2: a locked imaging benchmark, governance record and externally tested
encoder. If cross-site transfer is weak, the fallback is domain-specific adapters and federated or site-
stratified training rather than a single universal model.
Aim 2 — Link histological phenotypes to gene dynamics and treatment-resistant cellular states
Biological question: Which gene programmes, cell-state transitions and histological phenotypes distinguish
progression, treatment resistance and relapse from developmental or batch-related variation?
Starting data: Ongoing murine ependymoma and CPC work, paediatric tumour single-cell and histology data
available through my collaboration with Professor Richard Gilbertson’s laboratory, and public scRNA-seq
and spatial-transcriptomic benchmarks. Preliminary analyses recover known ependymoma genes, providing
feasibility evidence rather than a completed discovery.

Methodological advance: Graph-based embeddings and transformer representations will model changes in
gene–gene context across developmental and disease states. Histology encoders will quantify tissue
morphology and spatial organisation, allowing molecular programmes and cellular neighbourhoods to be
linked to observable tissue phenotypes through interpretable latent representations. The novelty lies in
linking dynamic gene function—not expression alone—to spatial and morphological evidence.
Benchmark and validation: Comparators will include differential-expression, pathway-enrichment,
pseudotime, established graph-based single-cell methods and histology-only encoders. Validation will test
recovery of established ependymoma and medulloblastoma programmes, replication across datasets and
agreement between molecular programmes, cellular neighbourhoods and histological localisation.
Experimental follow-up will be co-designed with biological collaborators and described as a commitment
only after the relevant assay, material and responsibility are formally agreed.
Delivery, people and decision point: Postdoctoral scientist 2 and PhD student 2 will lead histology, cellular
and spatial modeling, supported by the technician for sample metadata and reproducible processing. By
year 3: validated gene-dynamic and histology models and a prioritized set of replicated candidate
programmes linked to tissue context. If temporal labels are insufficient, the fallback is cross-sectional state-
transition modelling with explicitly limited causal claims.
Aim 3 — Integrate MRI, histology and molecular structures across biological scales
Biological question: Do MRI phenotypes encode histological, molecular and cellular states associated with
progression or treatment resistance, and are those links reproducible in unseen cohorts?
Methodological advance: Rather than concatenate modalities, the model will align the MRI representations
developed in Aim 1 with the histological, cellular and gene-regulatory representations developed in Aim 2,
using cross-attention and modality-aware objectives. Missing-modality training will permit inference when a
patient lacks one data type. The key output will be an auditable association between a macroscopic imaging
phenotype, tissue morphology and a replicated cellular programme—not an unsupported simulation of
treatment response.
Benchmark and validation: The integrated model will be compared with each unimodal model, late fusion
and matched multimodal baselines. Primary endpoints will be limited to labels actually available—such as
tumour class, progression/relapse status or treatment-related outcome—and prespecified before fitting.
External validation will use held-out public or collaborator cohorts; prospective clinical evaluation will be
pursued only after retrospective performance, calibration and governance criteria are met.
Delivery, people and decision point: The full team will integrate the modalities under my direction. By year 5:
an externally validated cross-scale framework, open evaluation protocols where permissions allow, and
experimentally prioritised hypotheses. If integration does not outperform the best unimodal model, the
deliverable will be a modular decision-support research tool that reports modality-specific evidence and
uncertainty rather than forcing fusion.

Robustness, responsible AI and statistical strategy
Before each study, label prevalence and missingness will determine the analysis population, split strategy
and sample-size or detectable-effect calculation. Batch and site effects will be measured explicitly;
harmonisation will be learned within training folds, never across the full dataset. Patient-level separation,
nested validation, site-held-out testing, sensitivity analysis and bootstrapped uncertainty will reduce leakage
and overinterpretation. Modality dropout and missingness indicators will address incomplete records.
Governance will include data minimisation, access controls, documented provenance, model cards,
subgroup performance, bias assessment and auditable versioning. Clinical utility will not be claimed without
independent and, where appropriate, prospective evaluation.

Manchester group’s agenda. Collaboration with Professor Pietro Liò’s group in Cambridge Computer Science
supports methodological exchange, while Professor Richard Gilbertson’s laboratory provides paediatric
tumour expertise, models and datasets for initial biological grounding. Professor Stephen Price’s imaging
dataset and my already-trained foundation model provide a practical starting point. These external links will
complement—not replace—Manchester-generated collaborations, data and validation.
At Manchester, I would develop specific prospective links with experimental researchers at CRUK MI, the
MCRC Biobank and clinical teams at The Christie. BRAINatomy 2 provides a particularly relevant opportunity:
the programme has received a second investment, is co-led by Professor Martin McCabe, and applies
advanced image-based analysis to understand the effects of paediatric proton therapy and protect sensitive
brain regions [7]. Professor Marianne Aznar’s imaging methodology and the programme’s links across The
Christie, Royal Manchester Children’s Hospital and international centres offer a concrete setting in which to
explore external imaging validation and treatment-related outcomes, subject to agreement with its
investigators. The ELLIS Unit Manchester, Centre for AI Fundamentals and Christabel Pankhurst Institute
provide complementary expertise in foundation models and trustworthy AI.
Programme management and funding strategy
The core-funded team will comprise two postdoctoral scientists, two PhD students and one technician. I will
lead scientific integration, recruitment, prioritisation and annual go/no-go reviews. Postdoctoral scientist
1/PhD student 1 will own imaging and interpretability; postdoctoral scientist 2/PhD student 2 will own
single-cell, spatial and cross-scale modelling; the technician will support data curation, governance and
reproducible pipelines. Years 1–2 establish benchmarks and Aim 1; years 1–3 deliver Aim 2 and locked cross-
scale protocols; years 3–5 deliver external validation and generalisation to adult glioma.
My funding trajectory begins with awarded EPSRC and UKRI access to high-performance computing,
including From MRI to Multi-Omics: An Explainable Foundation Model for Brain Cancer, and collaborator
status on two awarded biobank-data access applications. At Manchester, early benchmark and biological-
validation outputs will support a UKRI application, primarily to EPSRC, for the multiscale AI framework.
Subsequent applications will be staged around independently validated imaging and cell-state outputs
rather than the entire programme at once, complementing CRUK MI core support.
Expected contribution
By year 5, the programme will have tested a clear biological hypothesis, delivered externally evaluated
methods and produced replicated, interpretable links between tumour imaging, tissue structure and cellular
programmes. The long-term objective is not just an autonomous clinical co-pilot, but a trustworthy research
framework that helps cancer biologists and clinicians prioritise testable mechanisms and candidate
biomarkers while making uncertainty and evidential limits explicit.
