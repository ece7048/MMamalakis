                                           Teaching Statement
             Dr Michail Mamalakis | Associate Professor (Research and Education) in Computer Science
Teaching philosophy. My teaching is research-informed, application-driven and centred on helping students
become independent, critical and responsible computer scientists. I use authentic problems to connect
mathematical and computational principles with implementation, evaluation and societal consequences. In artificial
intelligence, particularly when methods are applied to health and science, students should learn not only how to
build a model, but also how to test whether it is robust, interpretable, reproducible and appropriate for its intended
use. My aim is to create an inclusive learning environment in which students with different disciplinary backgrounds
can develop technical confidence, ask difficult questions and apply computer science rigorously.

1. Evidence of teaching and curriculum leadership
Current module leadership at Cambridge. As an Affiliated Lecturer in the University of Cambridge Department of
Computer Science and Technology, I am Course Director and lead lecturer for Principles of AI-driven Neuroscience
and Translational Biomedicine (L205), offered to MPhil in Advanced Computer Science and Part III students (under
the supervision of Professor Pietro Lio, DoT). I organise the module, design and prepare the majority of its teaching,
deliver lectures, develop learning materials, support student projects and contribute to assessment. The module
comprises eight two-hour in-person sessions and brings together deep learning, graph neural networks,
transformers, agentic methods, explainable and mechanistic AI, neuroimaging, connectomics and translational
biomedicine (link of the course developed by me:https://ece7048.github.io/L205_site/ )
Curriculum design. I structure the curriculum so that foundational ideas lead progressively to advanced methods
and then to critical application. Students first establish a common conceptual vocabulary across AI and
neuroscience; they then examine modern architectures and interpretation methods; finally, they apply these tools to
realistic biomedical questions and evaluate limitations. This sequencing is particularly important in interdisciplinary
cohorts, where students may be strong in computer science but unfamiliar with neuroscience, or conversely
knowledgeable about biomedicine but less experienced in machine learning. I use worked examples, visual
explanations, short technical exercises and guided discussion to make assumptions explicit and to prevent
advanced terminology from becoming a barrier to participation.
Assessment and feedback. The module uses an authentic group mini-project alongside an individual presentation
and viva. This combination tests collaborative problem solving while preserving individual accountability and the
ability to explain technical decisions. I guide students to define a tractable research question, select appropriate
baselines, avoid data leakage, report uncertainty and distinguish predictive association from stronger scientific or
clinical claims. Feedback is provided through project discussions, office hours and assessment, with emphasis on
specific actions that improve technical reasoning, communication and reproducibility.
Breadth of teaching experience. At the University of Sheffield, I taught across programming, software engineering
and machine learning. My roles included laboratory demonstration, tutorials, project support and assessment in Java
Programming, C++ Programming, Introduction to Programming, Machine Learning and AI, ACS341 Machine Learning
and the COM6103 Team Software Project. In the team project, I supported Agile practice and Scrum-based
collaboration. These experiences developed my ability to explain core computational concepts, diagnose
misconceptions in code, support students with varied prior experience and assess both individual and team-based
work.

2. Research-informed and inclusive teaching practice
Connecting teaching and research. My teaching is informed by research in explainable AI, medical-image analysis,
foundation models, neuroscience and multiscale biomedical data. I use research examples to show students how
computer-science choices affect evidence: how split design can create leakage, how calibration differs from
discrimination, why an explanation may be unstable, and when a complex multimodal model fails to outperform a
simpler baseline. This approach develops methodological judgement rather than encouraging students to reproduce
fashionable architectures without adequate evaluation.
Active and authentic learning. I favour learning activities that require students to make and defend decisions. In
lectures and workshops, students compare alternative model designs, identify threats to validity, interpret
visualisations and discuss ethical or deployment constraints. In project work, I encourage reproducible workflows,
clear division of responsibilities and regular reflection on whether the evidence supports the claim. My invited
teaching at the Sano/Insigneo researcher training seminar, the Cambridge Centre for AI in Medicine Summer School
and the Trustworthy AI in Imaging workshop has further developed my ability to adapt technical material for
audiences ranging from computer scientists to clinicians and biomedical researchers.
Inclusive teaching. Interdisciplinary teaching requires deliberate inclusion. I introduce specialist language carefully,
provide multiple routes into a topic and make expectations for technical work transparent. I use examples from
different application areas and invite students to question data quality, representation, bias and the consequences
of model failure. For group work, I encourage defined roles, documented contributions and rotation of
responsibilities so that technical confidence or prior disciplinary status does not determine who participates. I would
continue to develop accessible digital resources, clear assessment criteria and opportunities for formative feedback,
while using student feedback and performance evidence to refine the curriculum.

3. Supervision, mentoring and student development
Research supervision. I currently lead and advise researchers across projects in MRI foundation models, paediatric
brain cancer, single-cell and histology-informed modelling, and neuroscience. I also co-supervise a PhD researcher
in the Department of Psychiatry and support postgraduate students working across computer science and clinical
disciplines. My supervision approach combines clear milestones with intellectual independence: students are
expected to understand the assumptions behind a method, maintain reproducible records, present negative as well
as positive results and develop a publication strategy appropriate to the maturity of the work.
Mentoring through collaborative challenges. I led a team of three PhD students in the 2023 AIIB MICCAI medical
computer-vision challenge, where the team achieved top-ten and top-five placements across segmentation and
survival-prediction tasks. The educational value of this experience extended beyond the ranking: students had to
plan experiments, allocate work, evaluate competing models and communicate decisions under time constraints. I
use this type of project to develop technical independence, teamwork and professional research practice.
Assessment and examining. My examining experience includes an internal PhD viva, a PhD first-year report and an
external MSc viva. These roles have strengthened my understanding of progression standards, constructive
questioning and fair assessment. They also inform how I prepare students to explain the originality, limitations and
contribution of their work.

4. Contribution to education at Birmingham
Teaching contribution. At Birmingham, I would contribute across undergraduate and postgraduate teaching in
artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, computer vision, programming and research methods. I could
also support interdisciplinary teaching in explainable AI, computational neuroscience, biomedical data science and
AI for scientific discovery. My experience across introductory programming, team software projects and advanced
postgraduate AI would allow me to teach at multiple levels and to connect foundational computer science with
contemporary research.
Proposed curriculum development. A module I would be well placed to develop is Trustworthy AI for Health and
Scientific Discovery. The module would treat healthcare as a demanding testbed for broadly applicable computer-
science principles rather than as a narrow application course. Topics would include robust evaluation, uncertainty
and calibration, explainability and mechanistic interpretability, multimodal learning, dataset shift, reproducibility,
fairness, governance and human oversight. Practical work would require students to compare baselines, audit failure
modes and communicate evidence to technical and non-technical audiences. The same principles would transfer to
cyber-physical, scientific and other high-consequence AI systems.
Educational leadership and enhancement. I would contribute to module and programme review, assessment
design, student recruitment, project supervision and the development of inclusive digital learning resources. I would
seek student input when refining project topics and assessment guidance, and would evaluate changes using
student feedback, attainment patterns and the quality of submitted work. I also intend to develop a stronger
education-scholarship profile by systematically evaluating research-informed teaching interventions and sharing
effective practice through internal forums and appropriate external venues. As my experience grows, I would support
colleagues through mentoring, shared teaching resources and collaborative curriculum development.
Student experience and employability. My teaching would emphasise skills that students can demonstrate beyond
the classroom: collaborative software development, reproducible experimentation, critical evaluation, technical
writing, oral defence and responsible decision-making. Where appropriate, I would develop projects with
researchers, clinicians or industry partners, ensuring that external engagement supports clear learning outcomes
and equitable access. This would help students understand how high-quality computer science is practised in
multidisciplinary settings and prepare them for research and professional careers.
5. Reflection and future development
My current profile combines broad teaching experience with recent module-level leadership in an advanced
interdisciplinary area. I recognise that sustained educational leadership requires continued development, evidence
and reflection. I therefore intend to strengthen my practice through peer observation, structured analysis of student
feedback and attainment, formal professional development in higher-education teaching, and dissemination of
evidence about what improves learning. My objective is to contribute as a generous academic citizen: teaching my
own modules well, supporting students and colleagues, and helping the School maintain curricula that are
contemporary, inclusive, intellectually demanding and connected to its research culture.
Overall, I would bring to Birmingham experience spanning foundational programming, software-team education,
advanced AI, neuroscience and translational biomedicine; evidence of curriculum and assessment responsibility;
experience mentoring postgraduate researchers; and a clear commitment to responsible, research-led and inclusive
education.
